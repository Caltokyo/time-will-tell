The Vertigo of Nothing.

There was a Japanese man named Taro Okamoto. Born in 1911, died in 1996. Painter, sculptor, and one of the defining figures of the Japanese avant-garde. His Tower of the Sun, built for Expo '70 in Osaka, is known as his signature work. On grounds dedicated to celebrating the future stood something like a mass of primordial life. For Okamoto, perhaps, the future and the primordial were never in contradiction.

In 1959, Okamoto travelled to Okinawa and gathered the experience into a book, Okinawa Bunkaron: Wasurerareta Nihon (On Okinawan Culture: The Forgotten Japan). Among the things that drew him most strongly were the utaki.

Utaki are traditional sacred sites found in Okinawa and the Nansei Islands. Spaces enclosed by forest, rock and trees. Some look like nothing special from the outside. Local communities have carried on prayers there connected to ancestors, deities and nature. Their forms and customs differ from place to place, and entry to some is restricted. An utaki is not simply a nature park. It is the very history of people continuing to treat a certain piece of nature as a special place.

What struck Okamoto was that neither great religious architecture nor lavish idols were required. There is nothing. At least, that is how it looks to someone from outside. And yet there is prayer. The more people build into a place, the more sacred it becomes — that is not necessarily so. If anything, perhaps something remained precisely because nothing extra was placed there.

Why do people go to places where there seems to be nothing? Probably because they do not think there is nothing. There is something there. Something beautiful. Something sacred. People go because that is what has been passed down to them.

But what is beautiful, what is sacred, is not easily known. There are trees. There are rocks. The wind is blowing. Perhaps that is all. You search anyway. You may visit once and feel nothing. You may understand suddenly, years later. And then you pass on what you found to the next generation. The next generation, too, searches with its own eyes. Generation after generation.

What is handed down here is not only knowledge about sacred places. It is the very attitude of searching, believing that a beauty not yet found is there. Utaki may also have been places that nurtured this kind of sensibility. But this is not merely an aesthetic experience. Outsiders are not free to replace, at will, the local faith, the relationships with ancestors, and the ways of prayer that belong to a place.

The information society brings hidden places to light. Satellite imagery, maps, social media, AI. Places unknown to outsiders begin to appear in search results. But knowing that a place exists is not the same as earning the right to enter it. Some cultures are protected by being made public; some relationships are damaged by it.

In the "place of nothing" that Okamoto looked upon, it was not that there was nothing. There was something the outside world did not yet understand. When the world becomes able to discover everything, what will become of a sacredness that has been protected by remaining undiscovered?
