Perhaps Religion Is an Old Technology.

Religion has something of a bad reputation. At least in parts of modern society that prize science and rationality, it does. Believing without evidence. Obeying authority. Clinging to old customs. It is true that religious authority has, at times, suppressed freedom and knowledge. But to dismiss a system that has lasted for thousands of years as a mere misunderstanding seems a little wasteful.

There is a Japanese manga called Chi.: On the Movements of the Earth. It is a story about the pursuit of knowledge of the Earth's motion, and its collision with authority and belief. It is fiction rather than historical record, but it raises a question: might the institutions that protect the truth of one era obstruct the truth of the next?

The advance of science widened the territory in which natural phenomena could be explained without religious authority. The spread of education, urbanisation, individual freedom and distrust of institutions have also changed the shape of faith. Of course, religion is not declining uniformly across the world. What has changed, in some societies, are the conditions under which religion is accepted as a self-evident authority.

So of what religion held, what should we let go of, and what should we keep? Let us think of religion as a kind of technology. A technology for detaching human attention from the everyday. A technology for moving emotion through music. A technology for altering the body's senses through architecture. A technology for marking time through ritual. A technology for carrying a community's memory across generations. And a technology for accepting what cannot be controlled.

Religion tends to be thought of as a system that gives people answers. But perhaps it also worked to raise people who keep searching for answers. Here there is a god. This is a sacred place. Even when told so, you do not see the god's form. Nor do you immediately understand what is sacred. Still, people visit, pray, look, and come back again and again. And the next generation searches in the same place.

Religion also has a history of dictating what to believe and what to find beautiful. So simply reviving the old institutions is not the answer. Could we nurture a sensibility that searches for beauty and the sacred, without demanding assent to doctrine and without forcing obedience to authority?

Today's algorithms also deal in human attention. Most of them, though, try to hold that attention for as long as possible. Certain religious spaces, by contrast, have returned human attention to oneself and to nature. The same technology of attention, pointed in a different direction.

There are people who seek the functions religion once served, without needing the form of religion. Can we nurture only the power to believe in, and search for, a beauty not yet visible — without forcing belief? There is someone who seems to be taking on that question through another entrance: contemporary art.
