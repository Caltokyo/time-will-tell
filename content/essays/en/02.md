The Land of Eight Million Gods Is Mass-Producing Characters.

Japan is a rather strange country. There are gods in stones. Gods in trees. Mountains, rivers, even kitchens are said to have their gods. At the same time, Japan exports fictional characters to the entire world: Totoro, Pikachu, the spirits of The Legend of Zelda, the Angels of Neon Genesis Evangelion, the uncontrollable force of life depicted in Akira. Are these two phenomena really unrelated?

Traditional Japanese religious sensibility carries a feeling that does not draw a hard line between humans and everything else. It senses will in nature. It senses presence in tools. It gives personality to what cannot be explained. Of course, this sensibility is not unique to Japan, and not every Japanese person feels it in the same way. Even so, Japanese stories return again and again to treating things that are not human as living beings.

What is Hayao Miyazaki's Totoro? An animal, a god, a spirit? It is hard to say. And yet, without quite knowing, we feel it would not be strange for it to exist. This sense — "I can't say what it is, but it might well be there" — matters.

Pikachu does not exist either. Yet people all over the world understand a small yellow creature that throws off electricity as a living thing: they name it, raise it, and are sad to part with it. Is the ability to grow attached even to something that does not exist merely a talent for entertainment? Or is it an extension of the sensibility that finds life in the world right in front of us?

Hayao Miyazaki, Hideaki Anno, Katsuhiro Otomo. Their modes of expression could hardly be more different, yet their work keeps returning to beings beyond human understanding or control. Gigantic bodies, uncanny life, forces that overwhelm civilisation. There is something here that resonates with what Taro Okamoto pursued: a raw intensity of life that tidy beauty alone cannot explain. The lines of direct influence cannot be gathered into a single lineage. Still, there is a shared question. Why do we find beautiful the things we cannot understand?

Look at a single tree. Some see only timber. Some feel hundreds of years of life. Some believe a god dwells in it. Physically, it is the same tree. What differs is the sensibility of the person looking.

The idea of eight million gods is not only a matter of number — of gods being everywhere. It is also an attitude: leaving open, in the world, the possibility that something beyond human understanding exists even within ordinary nature. The power to imagine life not yet seen, and the power to feel life in what already exists. The two may be closer than we think.

And there was an artist who, in modern Japan, gave that sensibility an almost unnervingly fierce expression.
